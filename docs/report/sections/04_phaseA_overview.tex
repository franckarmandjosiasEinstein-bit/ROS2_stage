\section{\phaseA{} --- Webots Prototype: Overview}
\label{sec:phaseA}

\subsection{Why a simulator-native prototype first}

The first decision of the project was not which algorithm to use but
\emph{where to make the first mistake}. Two routes were available:
start directly in ROS~2, where the target architecture lives, or start
in Webots \cite{michel2004webots}, where the robot model, the physics
and the sensors are supplied and a controller is a single Python file
with direct access to every device.

Webots was chosen, for three reasons that all proved correct in
hindsight:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{The KUKA YouBot is a first-class Webots model.} Its
        mecanum base, its five-DOF arm and its two-finger gripper are
        modelled with the manufacturer's dimensions. No URDF had to be
        authored, no inertias guessed, no controller plugin written
        before the first wheel turned.
  \item \textbf{One process, one debugger, no middleware.} A defect in
        a ROS~2 stack can be in the algorithm, in a QoS setting, in a
        remapping, in a transform, or in a launch file. In a Webots
        controller it can only be in the algorithm. Every hour spent
        debugging in \phaseA{} was spent on robotics rather than on
        plumbing.
  \item \textbf{Middleware is a re-architecture, not a rewrite.} If the
        algorithms are written without importing the simulator, moving
        them later is mechanical. Sec.~\ref{sec:architecture} shows that
        this held: A$^\star$, pure pursuit, the log-odds grid, the
        mecanum kinematics and the scan matcher were carried into
        \phaseB{} essentially unmodified.
\end{enumerate}

The cost of the choice is that \phaseA{} proves nothing about
distributed execution, message timing, or transform trees --- exactly
the class of problem that dominated \phaseB{}
(Sec.~\ref{sec:phaseB}).

\subsection{The \phaseA{} mission}

It is important to state plainly that \phaseA{} and \phaseB{} do not
solve the same task. \phaseA{} implements a \emph{crate-collection}
mission in a procedurally generated agricultural scene: the robot
explores an arena containing six planting basins
(\texttt{bassin\_1}\dots\texttt{bassin\_6}), discovers red crates with
its camera, plans a route to each, picks it up with the arm, stows it in
one of two body-frame slots on its rear plate, and delivers a full load
to a depot before returning to explore again.

\phaseB{} replaces the payload with strawberries growing on raised
gutters. The mission logic changes; the navigation, mapping, planning
and safety layers do not. That is precisely the domain-independence
requirement stated in Sec.~\ref{sec:phaseA}, and \phaseA{} is where it
was earned: nothing in \filename{navigation.py}, \filename{planning.py}
or \filename{mapping.py} mentions a crate.

\subsection{Incremental validation: fifteen controllers}

The distinguishing feature of \phaseA{} is methodological rather than
algorithmic. Instead of writing one controller and debugging it as a
whole, \emph{one single-purpose test controller was written per
subsystem}, in dependency order, and each had to work in isolation
before the next was started. Table~\ref{tab:phaseA_controllers} lists
all fifteen with their size.

\begin{table}[!t]
\caption{\phaseA{} controllers, in the order they were written}
\label{tab:phaseA_controllers}
\centering\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.30\columnwidth}rp{0.48\columnwidth}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Controller} & \textbf{LoC} & \textbf{Question it answers}\\
\midrule
\multicolumn{3}{@{}l}{\emph{Bring-up}}\\
\texttt{device\_scan}   &  61 & What devices does this robot expose,
                               and under what names?\\
\texttt{wheel\_test}    &  99 & Does each wheel turn, in the direction
                               and at the rate commanded?\\
\texttt{base\_test}     & 131 & Do the mecanum equations produce
                               forward, strafe and yaw correctly?\\
\texttt{odom\_test}     & 115 & Does wheel-encoder odometry track the
                               ground-truth pose?\\
\midrule
\multicolumn{3}{@{}l}{\emph{Perception}}\\
\texttt{lidar\_test}    & 149 & Does the lidar produce a usable
                               occupancy grid of a known scene?\\
\texttt{vision\_test}   & 110 & Are red crates segmented, and does the
                               ground projection land on them?\\
\texttt{localize\_test} & 160 & Does scan matching correct a
                               deliberately drifted odometry?\\
\midrule
\multicolumn{3}{@{}l}{\emph{Motion}}\\
\texttt{planner\_test}  & 115 & Does A$^\star$ return a collision-free
                               path on the inflated grid?\\
\texttt{nav\_test}      & 108 & Does pure pursuit follow that path
                               without cutting corners?\\
\midrule
\multicolumn{3}{@{}l}{\emph{Manipulation}}\\
\texttt{arm\_test}      & 110 & Does each arm joint reach its commanded
                               angle?\\
\texttt{grasp\_test}    & 170 & Does the full pick sequence close on a
                               crate and lift it?\\
\midrule
\multicolumn{3}{@{}l}{\emph{Integration}}\\
\texttt{world\_manager} & 185 & Supervisor: generate the scene, score
                               the mission, provide ground truth.\\
\texttt{my\_First\_ctrl} & 603 & The behaviour-tree mission that uses
                               every module above.\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Ten of the fifteen exist only to answer one question. This is
deliberate. A test controller that fails tells you which subsystem is
broken; an integrated controller that fails tells you only that
something is. The discipline is also what made the port to \phaseB{}
tractable, because each module arrived at the port already having a
known-good reference behaviour.

built, which is also the order of Table~\ref{tab:phaseA_controllers}:
the world and its supervisor (Sec.~\ref{sec:phaseA_world}), locomotion
