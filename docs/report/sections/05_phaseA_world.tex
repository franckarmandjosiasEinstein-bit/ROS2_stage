\subsection{World Modelling and the Procedural Greenhouse}
\label{sec:phaseA_world}

\subsubsection{Two worlds, two purposes}

\phaseA{} uses two Webots worlds. \filename{smart\_agriculture.wbt}
(258 lines) is the working scene: a \SI{10}{m} square arena with a
rectangular floor, four rows of planting basins, a robot start corner
and a depot corner. \filename{greenhouse.wbt} (100 lines) is a reduced
scene used by the single-subsystem test controllers, where a smaller and
fully deterministic layout makes a failure easier to attribute.

\subsubsection{Why the scene is generated, not authored}

The decisive design choice in \phaseA{} was to make the working scene
\emph{procedural}. A supervisor controller,
\filename{world\_manager.py} (185 lines), rebuilds the arena at the
start of every run from a fresh random seed, which it prints so that any
run can be replayed:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
seed = random.randrange(1_000_000_000)
random.seed(seed)
print(f"[world_manager] Greenhouse seed = {seed}")
\end{lstlisting}

The alternative --- one hand-authored scene --- is faster to build and
much more dangerous. A controller developed against a fixed layout
learns the layout. Waypoints get tuned until they happen to thread
between two particular basins; a planner inflation radius gets reduced
until one particular gap becomes passable. None of that survives contact
with a different arrangement, and none of it is visible while the scene
never changes. Randomising the scene makes overfitting fail loudly and
early, on the developer's own machine.

\subsubsection{What is randomised}

Table~\ref{tab:worldgen} lists the generation parameters. Three of them
carry most of the variability:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \param{POT\_SKIP\_PROB} $= 0.25$ deletes a quarter of the
        planting basins at random. The rows therefore have gaps, and
        those gaps change every run --- so the planner must actually
        search rather than follow a memorised corridor.
  \item \param{N\_CRATES} $\in [2,5]$ makes even the number of mission
        objectives unknown at start-up, which is what forced the mission
        layer to be a discovery loop instead of a fixed sequence
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:phaseA_mission}).
  \item \param{N\_WORKERS} $= 2$ adds moving obstacles that wander
        between random aisle points at \SI{0.30}{m/s}. They are the only
        dynamic elements in \phaseA{}, and they are what justifies
        re-running the mapper during navigation instead of mapping once
        and planning on a frozen grid.
\end{itemize}

The \param{KEEP\_CLEAR} mechanism deserves a note because it is the kind
of detail that is invisible until it bites. Nothing is spawned within
\SI{1.0}{m} of the robot's start corner $(-4.5, 4.5)$ or the depot
$(4.5, -4.5)$. Without it, roughly one run in ten began with a crate
spawned underneath the robot or a basin blocking the depot, and the
resulting mission failure looked like a planner defect rather than a
scene-generation artefact. A generator that can produce an impossible
scene will eventually produce one, on the run being demonstrated.

\subsubsection{The supervisor's second role: ground truth}

A Webots supervisor can read the true pose of any node in the scene
graph. \filename{world\_manager.py} uses this for two things that a
robot may not do:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Scoring.} It knows how many crates it created and where
        they are, so it can report how many the robot found, how many it
        delivered, and how many it missed --- an objective mission score
        that does not depend on the robot's own beliefs.
  \item \textbf{Reference data for perception.} It can answer
        ``what is really there?'' for a detection, which is how the
        vision module's false-positive rate was measured rather than
        estimated.
\end{enumerate}

This separation --- the robot's belief on one side, the simulator's
truth on the other, compared by a third party that participates in
neither --- is the single most valuable idea \phaseA{} contributed to
\phaseB{}. It reappears there, considerably expanded, as the
instrumentation layer of Sec.~\ref{sec:instrumentation}: three nodes
that continuously score pose, map and throughput against Gazebo ground
truth. The measurement discipline of this project starts here.

\subsubsection{Coordinate conventions}

The world frame is the XY plane with its origin at the arena centre,
$+x$ east and $+y$ north, following REP-103 \cite{rep103}. Body-frame
twists use $x$ forward, $y$ left, $\psi$ counter-clockwise. The
occupancy grid uses image conventions --- row indexes $y$, column
indexes $x$, origin at the top-left --- so a vertical flip is applied
whenever a grid is published or drawn. Getting that flip wrong produces
a map that is a perfect mirror of reality and looks entirely plausible
until a path is planned on it; it is checked explicitly in
\phaseB{}'s regression suite (Sec.~\ref{sec:regression}).
