\subsection{Perception: Lidar and Colour Vision}
\label{sec:phaseA_perception}

\phaseA{} carries exactly two exteroceptive sensors, and they answer two
different questions. The lidar answers \emph{``where can I go?''}; the
camera answers \emph{``what is that, and where is it?''}. Neither can
answer the other's question, and a substantial part of the mission logic
(Sec.~\ref{sec:phaseA_mission}) exists to reconcile the two.

\subsubsection{The lidar: what the sensor physically is}

The sensor is declared in the robot's \texttt{bodySlot} as

\begin{lstlisting}[style=xml]
Lidar {
  translation 0 0 0.2
  name "lidar"
  horizontalResolution 360
  fieldOfView 6.28
  numberOfLayers 1
  maxRange 12
}
\end{lstlisting}

Read literally, this is a single horizontal plane, \SI{0.20}{m} above the
base origin, sampled at 360 bearings over a full \SI{6.28}{rad}
revolution, with a \SI{12}{m} ceiling. Four consequences follow directly
from those five numbers, and every one of them shaped a later design
decision:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{It is a slice, not a view.} The lidar sees only what
        intersects one horizontal plane at one height. An obstacle below
        that plane --- a crate lying on the floor --- is invisible to it,
        which is precisely why crate detection had to be done in the
        camera and not in the lidar.
  \item \textbf{Its angular resolution is $1^\circ$.} Two returns
        \SI{2}{m} away are \SI{3.5}{cm} apart; at \SI{5}{m} they are
        \SI{8.7}{cm} apart. A gap narrower than the beam spacing at that
        range is simply not resolved, so the far field of a scan is
        systematically coarser than the near field. The mapper inherits
        this: a distant cell receives fewer and more widely spaced
        updates, and therefore converges more slowly --- which is the
        correct behaviour, not a defect.
  \item \textbf{It measures range, not free space.} A beam that returns
        \SI{3}{m} asserts one occupied point \emph{and} a \SI{3}{m}
        segment of emptiness behind it. Turning that pair of assertions
        into a map is the entire subject of
        Sec.~\ref{sec:phaseA_mapping}.
  \item \textbf{Its origin is the base centre, not the bumper.} The
        sensor sits at the geometric centre of a $0.58 \times
        \SI{0.38}{m}$ body. A raw range of \SI{0.28}{m} straight ahead
        therefore describes a point \emph{inside} the robot's own nose.
        This single fact caused the most damaging defect of the whole
        project, in \phaseB{}, and is dissected in
        Sec.~\ref{sec:safety}.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{A common misconception, stated and corrected}
A \SI{360}{\degree} lidar is often described as giving a robot
``awareness of everything around it''. It does not. It gives a
\emph{complete horizontal ring of distances at one height, with no
identity attached to any of them}. The scan cannot distinguish a wall
from a plant gutter from a moving worker; it cannot see above or below
its own plane; it has no memory from one scan to the next; and it
produces no notion of ``a corridor'' or ``a row'' --- those are
inventions of the mapper and the planner. Every behaviour that resembles
spatial understanding in this system is software built on top of a ring
of numbers.

\subsubsection{The camera: intrinsics as declared}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=xml]
DEF CAMERA_RGB Camera {
  translation 0.2 0 0.3
  rotation 0 1 0 0.5
  name "camera"
  width 160  height 120
  fieldOfView 1.2
}
\end{lstlisting}

The mount is fixed and known: \SI{0.20}{m} ahead of the base centre,
\SI{0.30}{m} above the body slot (about \SI{0.40}{m} above the floor),
pitched \SI{0.5}{rad} downward about the body $y$ axis. The image is
$160 \times 120$ with a horizontal field of view of \SI{1.2}{rad}. From
these the pinhole intrinsics follow with no calibration step at all:

\begin{equation}
f_x = f_y = \frac{W/2}{\tan(\theta_{\mathrm{fov}}/2)} = \frac{80}{\tan 0.6}
     \approx \SI{118.6}{px},
\label{eq:focal}
\end{equation}

with the principal point at $((W-1)/2,\,(H-1)/2)$. Square pixels are
assumed, which is exact for a Webots camera.

\subsubsection{Detection stage 1 --- brightness-invariant red segmentation}

Webots returns the image as a BGRA byte buffer, rows top to bottom. The
first version of the detector used an absolute margin: a pixel was red
if $R - G > 60$ and $R - B > 60$. It worked on the test bench and failed
in the arena, because the checkerboard floor is itself a brownish red.
The margin that rejected the floor also rejected any crate in shadow,
where all three channels scale down together.

The fix was to threshold on \emph{channel ratio} rather than difference:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M}(u,v) =
\big[R > 60\big] \wedge
\big[R \ge G\big] \wedge
\big[R \ge B\big] \wedge
\big[G < 0.33\,R\big] \wedge
\big[B < 0.33\,R\big].
\label{eq:redmask}
\end{equation}

The measured pixel statistics justify the constant. A crate pixel has
$G/R \approx 0.12$; a floor pixel has $G/R \approx 0.40$. A threshold of
$0.33$ separates them with margin on both sides and --- this is the point
of the change --- a ratio is invariant under illumination scaling, so
the same threshold holds in shadow where an absolute margin does not.
The implementation is four vectorised comparisons over the whole array:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
limit = self.red_ratio * r
return ((r > self.value_min) & (r >= g) & (r >= b)
        & (g < limit) & (b < limit))
\end{lstlisting}

\subsubsection{Detection stage 2 --- connected components}

The mask is grouped into blobs by 4-connectivity flood fill, implemented
with an explicit stack rather than recursion, because a $160 \times 120$
mask can produce a component deep enough to exhaust the Python recursion
limit. Components smaller than \param{min\_blob\_pixels} $= 4$ are
discarded as noise. That threshold is deliberately low: a crate at
\SI{3}{m} occupies only a handful of pixels in a $160 \times 120$ image,
and raising the threshold to a comfortable-looking 20 pixels made the
robot blind beyond \SI{1.5}{m}. Each surviving component contributes its
centroid $(\bar u, \bar v)$ in pixel coordinates.

\subsubsection{Detection stage 3 --- back-projection onto the ground plane}

A single camera cannot recover depth. It can, however, recover position
\emph{given a plane constraint}, and every crate rests on the floor at a
known height $z_g = \SI{0.05}{m}$. The centroid is first turned into a
ray in the camera frame (Webots convention: $+x$ forward, $+y$ left,
$+z$ up),

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{d}_c = \left(1,\; -\frac{\bar u - c_x}{f_x},\;
                         -\frac{\bar v - c_y}{f_x}\right),
\label{eq:ray}
\end{equation}

then rotated into the world by the camera's world orientation, which is
\emph{composed} rather than read from the simulator:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{R}_z(\psi)\,\mathbf{R}_y(\beta),
\qquad
\mathbf{p} = \begin{pmatrix}
  x_r + d\cos\psi\\[1pt] y_r + d\sin\psi\\[1pt] h_c
\end{pmatrix},
\label{eq:campose}
\end{equation}

with $\psi$ the robot yaw taken from its own pose estimate, $\beta =
\SI{0.5}{rad}$ the fixed mount pitch, $d = \SI{0.20}{m}$ the mount
offset and $h_c = \SI{0.40}{m}$ the camera height. Intersecting the
world-frame ray $\mathbf{d}_w = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{d}_c$ with the ground
plane gives the scale and the estimate:

\begin{equation}
t = \frac{z_g - h_c}{d_{w,z}},
\qquad
(x, y) = \big(p_x + t\,d_{w,x},\; p_y + t\,d_{w,y}\big).
\label{eq:groundhit}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Why the camera pose is composed and not read}
The supervisor API could return the camera node's true world transform
in a single call. Using it would have been shorter, and it would have
been cheating: the estimate would then be exact regardless of how badly
the robot's own localisation was drifting, and the perception error
measured in \texttt{vision\_test} would have been meaningless. Composing
the pose from the robot's \emph{own} estimate and a fixed, documented
extrinsic keeps the whole detection chain onboard, and makes
localisation error propagate into object position error exactly as it
would on hardware. This is the same discipline that later separates
\nodename{truth\_monitor} from the robot in \phaseB{}
(Sec.~\ref{sec:instrumentation}).

\subsubsection{Three rejection gates}

Equation~(\ref{eq:groundhit}) is numerically unstable near the horizon:
as $d_{w,z} \to 0$ the intersection runs to infinity, so a one-pixel
error in $\bar v$ can move the estimate by tens of metres. Three gates
bound the damage, and each was added in response to an observed failure
rather than anticipated:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \param{min\_ray\_down} $= 0.12$ --- reject rays not pointing
        down steeply enough. This is the horizon guard.
  \item \param{max\_range} $= \SI{3.0}{m}$ --- reject ground hits beyond
        the distance at which a crate is more than a few pixels wide.
  \item \param{arena\_bound} $= \SI{4.6}{m}$ --- reject any estimate
        outside the arena. A detection that lands inside a wall is wrong
        by construction, whatever produced it.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Two defects found only by testing against truth}

\texttt{vision\_test} spins the robot in place for \SI{30}{s} and prints,
once a second, the detections beside the supervisor's true crate
positions. Two defects surfaced that no amount of staring at the mask
would have revealed.

\textbf{One-off false positives.} A single frame in which a moving
worker's red-brown texture passed Eq.~(\ref{eq:redmask}) was enough to
register a phantom crate, after which the mission would drive across the
arena to collect nothing. The fix was \emph{temporal confirmation}: a
detection must be seen in several consecutive frames within a small
spatial window before it is admitted to the crate list. The cost is a
short delay before a real crate is accepted; the benefit is that
uncorrelated single-frame noise is rejected outright.

\textbf{Detections sitting on lidar obstacles.} The remaining false
positives clustered on the workers, which are both reddish and mobile.
The fix was cross-modal: at discovery time, a candidate whose projected
ground position falls close to a lidar return is rejected, because a
crate lies below the lidar plane and therefore \emph{cannot} produce
one. The two sensors disagreeing is itself the evidence. This is the
earliest appearance in the project of a principle that runs through all
of \phaseB{}: a belief supported by one sensor and contradicted by
another should be dropped, not averaged.

\subsubsection{What was measured}

Across the \texttt{vision\_test} runs the detector found every crate that
entered the field of view within \SI{3}{m}, and the residual position
error was dominated not by the image processing but by the yaw term in
Eq.~(\ref{eq:campose}): a $5^\circ$ heading error at \SI{2}{m} range
displaces the estimate by \SI{17}{cm}. That sensitivity is the reason
\phaseB{} devotes an entire subsystem to bounding heading drift
(Sec.~\ref{sec:slam}), and it is why the \phaseB{} fruit localiser
inherits both the strength of this design --- it works, cheaply, with no
training data and no labelled dataset --- and its weakness: it is never
better than the pose that feeds it.
