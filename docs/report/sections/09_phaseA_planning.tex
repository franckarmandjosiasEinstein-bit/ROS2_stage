\subsection{Planning and Path Following}
\label{sec:phaseA_planning}

Planning and following are two different problems and are solved by two
different pieces of code. The planner answers \emph{``what sequence of
free cells connects here to there?''} once, on a static snapshot of the
map. The follower answers \emph{``what should the wheels do in the next
\SI{32}{ms}?''} continuously, on a pose that is always slightly wrong.
Conflating them --- a single controller that both searches and steers ---
is a recurring failure mode in student robotics projects, and this
project deliberately avoided it from the first commit.

\subsubsection{A$^\star$ on the inflated grid}

The planner is A$^\star$ \cite{hart1968astar} over the 8-connected
$100\times100$ inflated grid produced in Sec.~\ref{sec:phaseA_mapping}.
Straight moves cost $1$, diagonal moves cost $\sqrt{2}$, and the
heuristic is the \emph{octile} distance, which is the exact cost of the
cheapest unobstructed 8-connected path:

\begin{equation}
h(a,b) = \sqrt{2}\,\min(\Delta_x,\Delta_y) + \big|\Delta_x - \Delta_y\big|,
\qquad
\Delta_x = |a_x - b_x|.
\label{eq:octile}
\end{equation}

The choice of heuristic is not cosmetic. Manhattan distance
($\Delta_x + \Delta_y$) \emph{over}-estimates the cost of a diagonal
move --- it charges $2$ where the true cost is $1.414$ --- and an
inadmissible heuristic destroys A$^\star$'s optimality guarantee, which
in practice shows up as paths that take an unnecessary dog-leg around a
basin. Euclidean distance is admissible but loose on a grid that cannot
move at arbitrary angles, and a loose heuristic expands more nodes than
necessary. Octile is both admissible and tight for this connectivity,
which is exactly the property one wants: optimal paths, minimal
expansions. On the \phaseA{} grid a typical query expands a few thousand
cells and returns in a few milliseconds --- fast enough to replan on
every mission event rather than clinging to a stale plan.

\subsubsection{Two robustness measures around the search}

The textbook algorithm assumes the start and goal are free cells. In a
running system, neither can be assumed, and both violations were
observed within the first hour of testing.

\paragraph{Nearest-free snapping}
The robot's own cell is frequently \emph{inside} the inflation band ---
that is what standing next to a wall means once obstacles have been
grown by the robot radius --- and A$^\star$ started from an occupied
cell returns no path at all. Likewise, a crate detected by the camera
sits on the floor beside a basin, and its cell is often inflated. The
planner therefore snaps both endpoints to the closest free cell by an
expanding square-ring search:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
for radius in range(1, max(rows, cols)):
    for dr in range(-radius, radius + 1):
        for dc in range(-radius, radius + 1):
            if max(abs(dr), abs(dc)) != radius:
                continue                 # ring, not disc
            if self._is_free(cand):
                return cand
\end{lstlisting}

The \texttt{continue} keeps the scan on the ring boundary, so cells are
visited in increasing Chebyshev distance and the first free cell found is
the nearest one. Snapping the goal is what makes ``drive to the crate''
mean ``drive as close to the crate as the configuration space allows''
rather than ``fail''.

\paragraph{Collinear smoothing}
A raw A$^\star$ result is one waypoint per cell: a \SI{6}{m} path is
sixty waypoints, fifty-eight of which lie on straight segments. The
follower does not benefit from them and the mission log becomes
unreadable. \texttt{\_smooth} keeps a cell only when the step direction
changes:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
if (dx1, dy1) != (dx2, dy2):
    out.append(cells[i])
\end{lstlisting}

Sixty waypoints typically collapse to five or six, with the geometry of
the path unchanged --- this is a pure compression of the representation,
not a shortcut. Genuine shortcutting (line-of-sight smoothing across the
grid, as in Theta$^\star$) was considered and rejected: it produces
paths that graze the inflation boundary, and the inflation margin is the
only collision guarantee the system has.

\subsubsection{Following the plan: pure pursuit}

The follower is pure pursuit \cite{coulter1992purepursuit,
snider2009steering}. Its state is a \emph{cursor} on the polyline,
$(i, t)$, meaning ``fraction $t$ along segment $i$''. Each tick:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item The cursor is advanced to the orthogonal projection of the robot
        onto the polyline, and \emph{never allowed to move backwards}
        (\texttt{self.\_cursor\_t = max(self.\_cursor\_t, t)}). Without
        that monotonicity, a robot pushed sideways past a corner will
        re-acquire an earlier segment and drive the path twice.
  \item A target is taken \param{lookahead\_distance} $= \SI{0.4}{m}$
        further along the polyline, walking segment by segment so the
        distance is measured \emph{along the path}, not as a chord.
  \item A velocity command toward that target is issued, braked
        proportionally within \param{brake\_distance} $= \SI{0.6}{m}$ of
        the final waypoint but never below \param{min\_speed} $=
        \SI{0.12}{m/s}$.
  \item Success is tested against the \emph{final waypoint} and the
        \param{position\_tolerance} $= \SI{0.15}{m}$, not against the
        cursor. Testing the cursor makes arrival depend on how the path
        happened to be discretised.
\end{enumerate}

The lookahead distance is the single tuning knob, and it trades two
failure modes against each other: too short and the robot oscillates
about the path, over-correcting on every tick; too long and it cuts
corners, which in a greenhouse means clipping a basin. The value
\SI{0.4}{m} was found by bisection against the \SI{1.2}{m} aisle width
of the \phaseA{} arena, and it is worth noting that the \phaseB{}
greenhouse, with \SI{0.80}{m} aisles, required it to be reduced to
\SI{0.32}{m} --- the parameter is a property of the environment, not of
the algorithm.

\subsubsection{The holonomic command law --- and the defect hidden in it}

\phaseA{} exploits the mecanum base directly. The velocity toward the
lookahead target is computed in the world frame, rotated into the body
frame, and issued \emph{together with} a proportional yaw command that
softly turns the base to face the direction of travel:

\begin{align}
v_x^{\text{body}} &= \phantom{-}\cos\psi\, v_x^{w} + \sin\psi\, v_y^{w},\\
v_y^{\text{body}} &= -\sin\psi\, v_x^{w} + \cos\psi\, v_y^{w},\\
\omega_z &= \mathrm{clip}\!\left(k_\psi\,
   \mathrm{wrap}(\mathrm{atan2}(\Delta y, \Delta x) - \psi),\,
   \pm 1.5\right).
\label{eq:crab}
\end{align}

This is legal, elegant and, in the \phaseA{} arena, effective: the base
translates immediately along the path while its heading catches up, so
no time is lost turning, and the lidar and arm end up pointing where the
robot is going. It was validated in \texttt{nav\_test} and used for the
whole of \phaseA{} without complaint.

It also contains a defect that \phaseA{} was structurally incapable of
revealing, and that cost several days in \phaseB{}. Because translation
and rotation are commanded simultaneously, the body points one way and
travels another --- it \emph{crabs}. Two consequences follow:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{The robot becomes wider.} A $0.58 \times \SI{0.38}{m}$
        body crossing an aisle at $45^\circ$ sweeps \SI{0.68}{m} of it
        instead of \SI{0.38}{m}. In a \SI{1.2}{m} \phaseA{} aisle that
        is harmless. In a \SI{0.80}{m} \phaseB{} aisle it is fatal.
  \item \textbf{The protective stop is aimed at the wrong obstacle.}
        \phaseB{}'s guard tests the corridor swept \emph{along the
        commanded direction}; when that direction is oblique, the test
        rectangle points into the gutter beside the robot instead of
        down the lane ahead of it, and the brake fires for something the
        robot was never going to reach.
\end{itemize}

The measured cost in \phaseB{} was \measured{46\%} of mission time spent
braked inside lanes the robot could have driven straight down. The
correction --- turn to face the target, then translate along the body
$x$ axis scaled by $\cos(\text{heading error})$, reserving holonomic
translation for the two manoeuvres that genuinely need it --- is
presented with its measurements in Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion}. It is
recorded \emph{here}, in the \phaseA{} chapter, because that is where
the defect was written, and because it is the clearest example in this
project of a design that is correct in one environment and wrong in
another for reasons no amount of code review would surface. Only a
narrower corridor and a time measurement exposed it.

\subsubsection{Validation}

\texttt{planner\_test} builds a grid from the supervisor, plans between
fixed start/goal pairs, and verifies that the returned polyline
traverses only free cells --- the property that inflation is supposed to
guarantee. \texttt{nav\_test} then drives those plans and reports final
position error against the GPS. Both are single-purpose controllers with
no mission logic, which is what makes a failure in either of them
attributable to one subsystem.
